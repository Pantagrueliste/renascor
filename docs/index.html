How the figures are made

About Renascor

Renascor is an open catalogue of digital editions of written documents from about 1450 to 1700: manuscripts, printed works, correspondence and inscriptions. Each record states who made the edition, which languages and years its texts cover, how large it is, how it is encoded and where to find it.

Scope. “Renaissance” is used here as a historical and cultural framework centred on Europe, particularly Western Europe, between 1450 and 1700. The catalogue also includes texts arising from Europe’s contacts with the rest of the world (trade, diplomacy, travel, migration, missions and colonial encounters), written on either side of these exchanges, in any language, and considered within their own traditions.

The catalogue points to editions and hosts no texts. It lists only editions that give a transcribed text, epigraphic editions included. Page images or photographs without transcription, uncorrected OCR and bibliographies are left out.

Why no paywalled editions? A digital text is most useful when it can be shared, checked, analysed and mined. Paywalls restrict that use and put scholars without institutional means at a disadvantage.

How records are made

  • Inclusion rule. Collections that provide freely accessible Renaissance texts are eligible even when their contents overlap. Each collection’s size is counted before content deduplication; deduplication applies separately to the Renascor Corpus. Subscription collections and directories without texts are excluded; exclusions are recorded with their reasons.
  • Contributions. Anyone can propose an edition or a correction through a form on GitHub, or by pull request. Each record is one JSON file in the public repository, checked against a published schema.
  • Review. The maintainer reviews every record before publication: that the edition exists, that it provides transcribed text from the period, and that it is described accurately.
  • Automated search. A weekly search uses Mistral Large 4, chosen for its multilingual capabilities and planned open-weight release, to propose candidate editions as pull requests. Every addition is reviewed by a human.
  • Document types. Each edition is classed by the kind of document its texts are: manuscripts (including archival documents and letters), printed works or inscriptions, or several of these when its texts mix them. The Type filter uses this classification.
  • Countries. Countries are the present-day countries where an edition’s documents were written or printed (ISO 3166 codes; England, Scotland and Wales are kept apart), as defined in schema/countries.json. They appear in each record and can be searched.

Origins and figures

This catalogue grew out of the Renascor Corpus Project, which aims to assemble the most complete possible corpus of Renaissance texts for AI training. The corpus is still a work in progress and has not yet been released.

The idea of a catalogue arose during that work but would have been shelved for lack of time. Rapid progress in agentic coding assistants made it possible to build within a reasonable time. Its purpose is to make clear which Renaissance texts are available in digital text form on the internet today.

Each collection’s word count includes its texts within 1450–1700, regardless of overlaps with other collections. The Renascor Corpus total removes duplicate texts, measuring the textual coverage of the corpus’s working harvest. The current corpus count is from June 2026. Partial collection counts and counting methods are explained in each record.

Periods are given as the first and last year of the texts; a single year is written once (1620, not 1620–1620) and “c.” marks an approximate date. The period filter keeps an edition when its period overlaps the years chosen. Periods recorded as centuries count as their hundred years (the 16th century as 1500–1599); periods given only in words are shown as recorded and are not matched by year filters.

The period graph offers an approximate view of textual coverage over time, including dates estimated from author lifespans.

For 41 editions the record also gives the date of the last change reported by the host, such as the last push to a GitHub repository or the last update of a Zenodo record.

Structured text data in JSON files. 1 edition.
HTML
Texts published as web pages. 42 editions.
Wikitext
Wiki markup, as used by Wikisource. 9 editions.
Plain text
Text files without structural markup. 13 editions.
Markdown
Text with lightweight Markdown markup. 1 edition.
Other
Another format, such as TSV or CoNLL-U. 18 editions.

The encoding is the richest publicly available text format: TEI XML first, then other XML, JSON, HTML, wiki markup, plain text and Markdown. Internal search or storage formats do not establish a public download format. Where an edition’s files can be downloaded (repository, download page or dataset record), the record links them, and the Files filter lists editions under every recorded download format.
